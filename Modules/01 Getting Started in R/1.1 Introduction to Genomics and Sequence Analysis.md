## Defining genomics

> [!definition] Genomics
> - **genomics**: a field of biology that studies all the DNA in an organism, including how genes and other functional elements interact with each other
### Types of genomics research
#### Structural genomics
- **structural genomics**: field of genomics focused on the description of three-dimensional structure of proteins encoded in a genome
- Includes gene mapping, DNA sequencing, and sequence annotation
- Structural genomics answers questions such as:
	- *What is the entire DNA sequence (genome) of an organism?*
	- *How many genes are there, and where are they located?*
	- *What are the predicted functions (annotations) of those genes?*
	- *What is the architecture of those genes?* 
		- Identification of introns, exons, regulatory elements
	- *What is the architecture of non-coding regions?* 
		- Sequencing and identification of telomeres, centromeres, repeating DNAs, transposable elements, ect...
#### Functional genomics
- **functional genomics**: field of genomics focused on the description of gene and gene product functions and interactions
	- Includes expression profiling, transcriptomics + proteomics, and mutant analysis
	- Focuses on understanding how to identify gene expression and regulation
- Functional genomics answers questions such as:
	- *What genes are being expressed at a given time/under a certain condition?*
		- Allows for comparison of gene expression at different time points, tissue types, or environmental conditions (like infection, apoptosis, etc...)
	- *What are the dynamics of gene expression under different conditions?*
	- *What regulators are responsible for changes in gene expression?*
	- *Is there epigenetic control of differential gene expression?*
	- *What genes are responsible for phenotypes of interest?*
		- Typically involves high-throughput genetic screens
	- *Which regions of the genome interact with each other?*
		- Develops a mechanistic understanding of regulation; who is regulating what?
#### Comparative genomics
- **comparative genomics**: field of genomics focused on comparing genome sequences across species, addressing genome evolution and selection
- Comparative genomics answers questions such as:
	- *How different are genome sequences between organisms or between individuals within the same species?*
	- *What genomes are the most conserved and what are the newest?*
	- *What regions of the genome appear to be under strong or weak selection?*
	- *How do new mutations arise, and how to new mutations and standing genetic variation contribute to evolution?*
	- *What is the genetic basis of complex traits?*
		- Typically involves genome mapping and genome-wide association studies (GWAS)
## Technical foundations of genomics
- Key innovations that form the technical foundations of genomics:
	- Recombinant DNA technology
	- Molecular cloning
	- PCR amplification
	- Hybridization techniques
	- DNA sequencing
### Polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

> [!definition] PCR amplification
> - **PCR amplification**:  "molecular copy machine"; laboratory technique used to amplify copies of a DNA sequence for study

- PCR was made feasible by the discovery of Taq polymerase and the development of automated thermal cycling
- **Taq polymerase**: a heat-stable DNA polymerase isolated from bacteria that live in geysers in Yellowstone
	- Taq polymerase is taken from *Thermus aquaticus* bacteria
	- Can be expressed in E.coli and isolated by boiling the cells (killing everything that's not heat-stable)
- Components of a PCR amplification:
	- Buffer, pH optimized for Taq function
	- dNTPs (nucleotide bases used to synthesize new DNA)
	- Template DNA, which will be amplified
	- Primers (small, specific DNA oligomers)
	- Taq, or another thermostable polymerase
- PCR machines preform an automated thermal cycling process:
1. **Denaturation** | High heat denatures DNA strands, rendering it single-stranded
2. **Annealing** | The temperature is lowered, allowing the primers to bind to the template at a lower temperature
3. Extension | Taq extends DNA strands starting at the primers
- Repetition of the cycling process creates exponentially larger amounts of DNA
### DNA sequencing
#### Sequencing methods
##### First generation sequencing
- DNA was very difficult to analyze until the development of first-generation sequencing methods

> [!definition] First generation sequencing
> - **Sanger sequencing**: first generation sequencing; 

- Sanger sequencing can be thought of as PCR with a single primer (which results in linear amplification of the DNA)
- Sanger sequencing is based on the principle that DNA extension is stopped by dideoxynucleotides (ddNTPs)
	- During DNA extension, the 3' hydroxyl group forms the phosphodiester bond that makes up the DNA backbone
	- ddNTPs lack the 3' hydroxyl group, making it impossible to extend

> [!process] Automated sequencing
> 1. DNA is placed on gel or in a capillary
> 2. As fragments of DNA move off the gel in order of their size, they pass through a laser beam
> 3. The color that each fragment fluoresces is recorded and used to identify the nucleotide
##### Second generation sequencing
- **second generation sequencing**:
	- Developed by Solexa
- Massively parallel/high throughput sequencing

> [!process] Sequencing by synthesis
> 1. A library of DNA fragments is generated, each of which have adapter oligonucleotides attached to them
> 2. The library is loaded onto a flow cell containing a law of primers complimentary to the adapters
> 3. DNA molecules are captured by primers, and fold over to form a bridge
> 4. The primer-bound DNA molecules are replicated using PCR, resulting in clusters of DNA across the flow cell
> 5. Clusters are sequenced using reversible fluorescent terminators (i.e DNA polymerase adds a single base to each cluster at each time)
> 6. An image of the flow cell is generated, and the fluorescent color at each cluster indicates the nucleotide
> 7. The reversible fluorescent terminator is cleaved, and the process of adding and imaging a reversible terminator is repeated for 50-150 cycles

- Second generation sequencing outputs millions of short single-end or paired-end reads that can be assembled into a genome
##### Third generation sequencing
- **third generation sequencing**: long-read sequencing; 
	- Often generates thousands of long reads
	- Less accurate than short-read sequencing; 99.0 % accuracy vs +99.9%
	- Useful for end-toend chromosome sequencing
- Includes PacBio and Oxford nanopore sequencing

> [!process] PacBio sequencing
> 1. DNA is circularized by ligating adapters to each strand
> 2. DNA polymerase and a primer, which is homologous to the adapter, is added to the DNA
> 3. Circularized DNA, primers, and DNA polymerase are aded to a flow cell and immobilized in a nanowell
> 4. Fluorescent nucleotides are added, and the incorporation of each nucleotide emits a measurable light
> 5. The circular DNA sequence is read repeatedly for increased acuracy

> [!process] Oxford nanopore sequencing
> 1. Protein nanopores are embedded in a synthetic membrane
> 2. A helicase unwinds the DNA and feeds one strand through the poor
> 3. A current is applied, and the distinct electric disruption caused by the passage of each nucleotide base is recorded

#### Sequencing workflow

> [!process] "Standard" illumina sequencing workflow
> 1. Obtain nucleic acids (DNA or RNA)
> 2. Generate Illumina library
> 	1. Fragment the nucleic acids in ~300 bp segments
> 	2. Ligate adapters
> 	3. Use PCR to amplify the library
> 3. Sequence the nucleic acids on a flowcell, producing reads and quality scores for each sample
> 4. Preform quality control analysis of each sample
> 5. Align reads to a known reference genome/transcriptome (or assemble the genome de novo, if it's completely new)
> 6. Assign reads to genes (or generate annotations de novo to identify genes)
> 7. Preform downstream analysis, which may include determining the number of read counts that map to a gene or identifying allelic differences between samples

- If necessary, you can use barcoded (i.e unique, known) adapters
### Technical challenges in genomic analysis
- Genomic data is typically very large, resulting in large storage requirements and high computational times
- Developing robust computational algorithms that can acurately captyre the complexity of biological relationships is difficult
- Genomic algorithms must be reproducible/verifiable (both at the bench and the computer)
#### Batch effects
- **batch effects**: technical variation that occurs between experiments with the same parameters due to differences in sample handling between batches
	- Might result from: 
		- Libraries generated by different researchers
		- RNA isolated by different methods
		- Sequencing on different lanes of the flowcell

> [!example] Batch effects
> - A study reported that gene expression clustered by species rather than tissue
> 	- Contradicted previous research
> 	- Contradicted the assumption that major developmental pathways are conserved across mammals
> - Data turned out to have been experiencing a batch effect
> 	- Each species was assigned to a different flowcell
> 	- Errors in the study design resulted in correlation by flowcell (i.e species)

- If the confounder completely correlated with the experimental treatment, you won't be able to correct for it

#### Best practices
- Determine how many samples are needed for comparison
	- In many cases, a minimum of three biological replicates per experimental group (from genetically identical individuals)
	- Preform power analysis to determine an appropriate population size
- Capture day-to-day variation
	- May involve collecting different replicates on different days
- Randomize how samples are processed
